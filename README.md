# Multi-Orbit Scientific Data Relay Network

**IBM AI Builders Challenge — Space Exploration**

An AI-powered satellite data-routing prototype for moving scientific data through a dynamic multi-orbit network where connectivity changes over time, transmission opportunities are limited, and mission data has different priorities and deadlines.

---

## Problem Statement

Space missions can generate large volumes of scientific data, but transmitting that data back to Earth is not always straightforward.

A research spacecraft may not have direct ground contact when data is created. Relay opportunities appear and disappear as satellites move, bandwidth is limited, packets have different priorities and deadlines, and transmissions can fail.

This creates a routing problem that is fundamentally different from routing on a static terrestrial network.

Our project asks:

> **Can an AI routing policy make effective forwarding decisions in a dynamic satellite relay network, and how does it compare with a deterministic temporal-routing baseline?**

A successful system must consider:

- whether a physical contact is available;
- whether a complete transmission fits inside that contact window;
- remaining link capacity;
- payload size;
- science priority;
- delivery deadline;
- stochastic transmission risk;
- which relay or ground receiver should be selected next.

The objective is therefore not simply to find the shortest geometric path. It is to make useful routing decisions in a changing, resource-constrained environment.

---

## Solution Description

We built a physical multi-orbit routing simulation in which science-data bundles are generated across three research satellites and routed through LEO and GEO relay satellites toward one of three operational ground receivers.

### Final network

| Role | Count |
|---|---:|
| Research satellites | 3 |
| LEO relay satellites | 6 |
| GEO relay satellites | 2 |
| Ground receivers | 3 |
| **Total nodes** | **14** |

The system models:

- orbital propagation;
- time-dependent physical contact windows;
- bandwidth and contact capacity;
- packet size, priority and deadlines;
- seeded stochastic transmission failures;
- retries and rerouting;
- delivery to any valid ground receiver.

Each data bundle is generated by one of the three research satellites and moves through the network hop-by-hop.

If a transfer fails, the packet remains at the sender. Time advances to the detected failure point, some contact capacity may already have been consumed, and the routing policy must retry or select another route.

The final application includes an interactive dashboard for exploring:

- the orbital network;
- currently available physical links;
- generated and active packets;
- delivered packets;
- the route taken by a selected packet;
- failed transfer attempts;
- packet metadata;
- spacecraft and ground-receiver metadata;
- benchmark results;
- locked experiment metadata.

## AI Approach and Architecture

The project compares two routing approaches operating on the same physical network and stochastic transfer model.

### Temporal earliest-arrival routing

The Temporal baseline searches the time-dependent ContactPlan for a feasible route toward any of the three ground receivers.

It accounts for when contacts start and finish and whether a transmission can fit fully inside the available contact window.

### MaskablePPO reinforcement learning

The AI router uses **MaskablePPO**, a reinforcement-learning algorithm that selects the next forwarding action from the actions that are currently feasible.

The PPO policy operates **hop-by-hop** rather than pre-programming an entire end-to-end route.

The final policy was trained with:

- **14 routing actions**
- **158 observation features**
- **2,000,000 training timesteps**
- **4 parallel environments**
- **32 bundles per training episode**
- **training seed 42**

Invalid actions are masked so that the policy selects only from feasible routing actions.

### System architecture

```text
Science-data generation
        |
        v
3 research satellites
        |
        v
Orbital propagation
        |
        v
Physical ContactPlan
        |
        +------------------------------+
        |                              |
        v                              v
Temporal earliest-arrival       MaskablePPO policy
routing                         AI next-hop decision
        |                              |
        +---------------+--------------+
                        |
                        v
               Feasible next hop
                        |
                        v
          Capacity-aware transmission
                        |
                        v
          Stochastic success / failure
                        |
              +---------+---------+
              |                   |
              v                   v
          Retry/reroute        Delivered
                         to one of 3 ground receivers
```

Both algorithms use the same final execution engine.

A transfer must satisfy the physical contact constraint:

```text
departure_time + transmission_time <= contact_end_time
```

This ensures that the simulation does not count transmissions that would extend beyond the available contact window.

## Final Benchmark

The locked final evaluation uses:

- **20 held-out paired traffic scenarios**
- **500 science bundles per scenario**
- **10,000 evaluated bundles per algorithm**
- identical traffic-seed families for both algorithms;
- identical stochastic-seed families for both algorithms;
- pure PPO with no Temporal fallback in the headline comparison.

| Metric | Temporal | PPO |
|---|---:|---:|
| Delivery ratio | **75.28%** | 62.16% |
| On-time delivery | **71.08%** | 59.47% |
| Priority-weighted timely delivery | **67.21%** | 57.08% |
| Mean latency of successful deliveries | 216.3 s | **181.3 s** |
| Mean hops | **2.23** | 3.39 |
| Transfer failure rate | 4.39% | **3.40%** |

The results show a useful trade-off rather than a single universal winner.

**Temporal routing is more complete:** it delivers more packets and satisfies more deadlines.

**PPO has lower average latency among the packets it successfully delivers:** approximately **181.3 seconds**, compared with **216.3 seconds** for Temporal — around **35 seconds lower**, or roughly **16% lower mean successful-delivery latency**.

PPO also recorded a lower per-attempt transfer-failure rate in the final benchmark.

Because the two algorithms do not successfully deliver exactly the same set of packets, the latency result should be interpreted as the mean latency among each algorithm's successful deliveries rather than proof that PPO is faster for every identical packet.

Committed benchmark evidence is available in:

```text
artifacts/final_experiment/benchmark/
├── summary.json
├── seed_metrics.csv
└── bundle_results.csv
```

The benchmark can be reproduced with:

```bash
python run_final_benchmark.py
```

## How IBM Bob Was Used

IBM Bob was used as a primary AI-assisted development tool throughout the project.

The project initially consisted of several technical components that were developed independently:

1. the **orbital simulator**, responsible for spacecraft motion and network geometry;
2. the **reinforcement-learning environment and PPO routing model**;
3. the **Temporal network-routing implementation**.

IBM Bob assisted the team during development of these components by supporting implementation, iteration and debugging.

### Integrating the independent components

Bob's most important role was helping us create the **integration layers** that brought the independently developed components into one coherent system.

The integration had to connect:

```text
Orbital simulator
        ↓
Physical contact generation
        ↓
Temporal / PPO routing
        ↓
Capacity + stochastic transfer execution
        ↓
Benchmarking
        ↓
Interactive frontend
```

This required the separate systems to agree on node identities, packet state, routing actions, contact windows and transfer semantics.

IBM Bob helped us reason through these interfaces, identify incompatibilities and implement the adapters required to make the components operate together.

### Integration and regression testing

IBM Bob was also used to help design tests that verified successful integration.

These tests were intended to make it clear when the project had been downloaded, configured and integrated correctly.

They check areas including:

- component compatibility;
- physical contact-window validity;
- routing and transfer behaviour;
- experiment configuration consistency;
- PPO checkpoint compatibility;
- benchmark reproducibility;
- frontend/backend integration;
- final release integrity.

The repository includes a final release-verification process so a user can confirm that the expected model, configuration and benchmark artifacts belong to the same experiment before running the prototype.

### Frontend development and rapid prototyping

IBM Bob was also used extensively during development of the interactive frontend.

It enabled rapid prototyping and experimentation with different ways of presenting a large amount of simulation information without overwhelming the user.

Bob assisted us in iterating on:

- the live orbital visualization;
- network-topology presentation;
- packet inspection;
- route and transmission-failure visualization;
- benchmark presentation;
- satellite inspection;
- metadata organization;
- progressive disclosure of technical details.

A major frontend goal was to expose the large amount of metadata attached to the simulation — packets, satellites, contacts, routing attempts and experiment configuration — in a concise and understandable way.

IBM Bob helped us rapidly test alternative layouts and interaction patterns until this information could be accessed without dominating the main simulation view.

Overall, IBM Bob acted as an AI development partner across **component implementation, system integration, debugging, testing, rapid prototyping and frontend design**.

## Why This Matters

Future missions may involve distributed spacecraft, relay satellites and increasingly autonomous operations.

Such systems cannot always rely on continuous communication or a single direct path to Earth.

This prototype provides an environment for exploring questions such as:

- when should scientific data be relayed instead of waiting for direct ground contact?
- how should high-priority packets be handled?
- can an AI policy learn useful decisions from changing contact opportunities?
- what trade-offs exist between delivery completeness and delivery latency?
- how should routing react when a planned transmission fails?

The project demonstrates how AI can become part of the decision-making layer of a space communications system rather than simply being used to analyse data after it reaches Earth.

## Scientific Scope and Limitations

This project is a **simulation and proof of concept**, not an operational satellite network.

Orbital positions and contact opportunities are derived from the configured orbital geometry.

The following are simulation assumptions rather than measurements from an operational mission:

- traffic distributions;
- packet priorities;
- stochastic failure parameters;
- spacecraft-health assumptions;
- weather-risk values;
- link characteristics.

The final routing topology is:

```text
3 research spacecraft
        ↓
6 LEO relays + 2 GEO relays
        ↓
3 operational ground receivers
```

The ground receivers are explicit routing destinations.

Any Earth-surface points used in earlier constellation-coverage experiments are separate from the final routing network and are not treated as ground stations.
